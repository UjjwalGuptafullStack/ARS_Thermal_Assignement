\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We measure the surface urban heat island of Mumbai two ways. Part~A
uses a Landsat~8 day/night pair from 8--9~October~2015 at
\SI{30}{\metre}. Part~B uses MODIS eight-day composites from
2019--2023 at \SI{1}{\kilo\metre}, across four seasons, on both Terra
and Aqua, day and night. Both use the same administrative boundary and
the same land-cover definition of urban.

Daytime UHI is strongly seasonal
and changes sign: Terra MODIS gives $-2.72$\,\si{\degreeCelsius} in
pre-monsoon and $+1.67$\,\si{\degreeCelsius} in post-monsoon.
Night-time UHI is positive in every season, between $+1.38$ and
$+2.80$\,\si{\degreeCelsius}. The Landsat pair gives
$+2.39$\,\si{\degreeCelsius} by day and $+2.13$\,\si{\degreeCelsius}
by night once emissivity is corrected, the latter agreeing to within
\SI{0.04}{\degreeCelsius} with MODIS for the same season and year. The
central finding is that Mumbai's daytime surface heat island is not a
fixed property of the city but a seasonal one, driven largely by how
hot the surrounding countryside becomes, while the night-time heat
island persists year-round.
\end{abstract}

% =============================================================
\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================

Mumbai is
among the most densely populated cities in the world, built on a
coastal peninsula with limited room to expand outward. Replacing
vegetated and bare ground with concrete, asphalt and roofing changes
the surface energy balance: built materials store more heat during the
day and release it slowly after sunset, while the loss of vegetation
removes the evaporative cooling that would otherwise moderate daytime
temperatures. The consequences are practical --- heat stress and
heat-related mortality, higher building cooling demand and electricity
consumption, degraded outdoor thermal comfort, and interaction with
air pollution.

This report measures the surface urban heat island, not the canopy-layer
air-temperature heat island the term often implies. The two are related
but not interchangeable: surface temperature responds faster and varies
more sharply over short distances, and over built surfaces by day it can
exceed air temperature by several degrees. Everything reported here is a
radiometric surface measurement.

Two questions are addressed. Part~A asks how the heat island differs
between day and night, using a Landsat~8 pair acquired one day apart in
October 2015 at \SI{30}{\metre}. Part~B asks how it varies across
seasons, using MODIS eight-day composites from 2019--2023 at
\SI{1}{\kilo\metre} on two platforms. Both parts share one boundary and
one definition of urban and rural, so that differences between them
reflect sensor and season rather than method.

\paragraph{A note on the label.}
GADM names this polygon ``Mumbai City'' (\texttt{IND.20.17\_1}), but
its bounding extent runs from \ang{18.891}\,N to \ang{19.269}\,N and
the mapped area is \SI{480.1}{\kilo\metre\squared}. The Mumbai City
district proper --- the island city --- ends near \ang{19.03}\,N and
covers roughly \SI{157}{\kilo\metre\squared}. The polygon used here
therefore spans both Mumbai City and Mumbai Suburban districts, that
is, Greater Mumbai, despite carrying the narrower GADM label. It is
referred to as Greater Mumbai throughout this report, and the GADM
identifier is given above so the exact polygon can be retrieved.

\paragraph{Why this level, and what the alternatives would have done.}
Three
definitions of ``Mumbai'' were available. The municipal limit of
Greater Mumbai, administered by the MCGM, is the polygon used here.
The Mumbai Metropolitan Region (MMR) extends much further, taking in
Thane, Kalyan, Navi Mumbai and a large area of open land between them.
A satellite-derived built-up extent would be a third option, drawing
the boundary from the imagery rather than from administration.

The municipal limit was chosen because it matches the unit that
actually governs the surface: building regulation, land use and green
space in Greater Mumbai are set by one authority, so the boundary
corresponds to a coherent management area rather than an arbitrary
line. It is also the unit against which any policy response would be
framed.

The alternatives would have moved the result in opposite directions.
MMR would pull a great deal of non-urban land inside the boundary ---
the region is far from uniformly built --- diluting the urban mean
with vegetated and agricultural pixels and lowering the measured UHI.
It would also consume much of the land that here serves as the rural
reference, forcing the ring further out. A built-up extent would do
the reverse: by excluding parks, water bodies and undeveloped land
from the urban zone it would raise the urban mean and increase the
measured UHI, at the cost of a boundary that shifts with the
classification threshold and has no administrative meaning.

\subsection{Urban and rural zones}

Zones come from ESA WorldCover v200 (2021) \cite{worldcover}, a
\SI{10}{\metre} global product with eleven classes.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Urban} --- built-up (class 50) inside the boundary.
        \SI{204.72}{\kilo\metre\squared}.
  \item \textbf{Rural} --- tree cover, shrubland, grassland, cropland
        and bare/sparse (classes 10, 20, 30, 40, 60) in a ring from
        \SI{5}{\kilo\metre} to \SI{20}{\kilo\metre} beyond the
        boundary. \SI{962.13}{\kilo\metre\squared}.
\end{itemize}

The \SI{5}{\kilo\metre} inner gap keeps the peri-urban fringe and the
city's thermal plume out of the reference. The \SI{20}{\kilo\metre}
outer limit keeps the ring in the same coastal climate. Both are
varied in Section~\ref{sec:ringsens}.

\paragraph{Classes that are neither clearly urban nor clearly rural.}
Three WorldCover classes are neither clearly urban nor clearly rural.
Herbaceous wetland (90) and mangrove (95) were excluded from the rural
reference: Mumbai's creeks are mangrove-lined and tidally inundated, so
these surfaces hold standing water for much of the tidal cycle and
behave thermally like water rather than land. They were not moved into
the urban zone either, since they are not built. Moss and lichen (100)
and snow and ice (70) do not occur in the study area. Bare and sparse
vegetation (60) was kept in the rural reference: it is genuinely
non-built, and excluding it would have biased the reference toward
vegetated, and therefore cooler, surfaces.

\paragraph{Mask erosion.}
\label{sec:erosion}

No mask erosion (shrinking each zone inward by a fixed radius to
discard mixed-pixel edges) was applied to either the urban or the
rural mask. A different mechanism was used instead to achieve the same
purpose. The urban zone is bounded by the administrative edge rather
than eroded, and contamination of the \emph{rural} reference by the
urban fringe --- the effect erosion is normally used to prevent --- is
handled by the \SI{5}{\kilo\metre} inner gap of the ring
(Section~\ref{sec:ringsens}): rather than eroding a rural mask that
touches the city, the ring is defined so that it never touches the
city in the first place.

The ring's inner and outer radii were therefore treated as the
parameter to sweep, in place of an erosion radius, and the same
question the brief asks of erosion --- how far the buffer extends and
how much UHI moves as a result --- is answered by
Table~\ref{tab:ringsens} and Table~\ref{tab:landsat_ring}: moving the
inner edge from \SI{2}{} to \SI{10}{\kilo\metre} shifts MODIS UHI by
up to \SI{0.50}{\degreeCelsius}, and the same sweep on Landsat shifts
it by at most \SI{0.18}{\degreeCelsius}. The urban mask was left
un-eroded because it is defined by the administrative boundary rather
than by a buffer, so there is no equivalent contamination on that side
to remove.

The reason is physical. Water has very high heat capacity and thermal
inertia, so its surface temperature barely moves over the diurnal
cycle while land swings widely. Leaving it in would anchor the rural
mean to a surface that cannot respond to the forcing being measured.

The bias would also change sign between day and night: the sea is
cooler than land by day, which would inflate daytime UHI, and warmer
by night, which would suppress night-time UHI. Section~\ref{sec:watertest}
measures this rather than assuming it.

The two scenes are one day apart, which is what makes the day/night
comparison meaningful --- a larger gap would mix the diurnal signal
with changing weather.

Night-time Landsat acquisitions are rare and irregular. They are
ascending-node passes, so the same ground location carries a different
WRS-2 path/row by night than by day, and they are frequently filed in
Tier~2 rather than Tier~1 because of looser geometric accuracy. Both
tiers were therefore searched, and because night scenes cannot be
chosen freely, the daytime scene was picked \emph{after} --- the
nearest clear daytime acquisition to the identified night scene,
rather than the other way round.

This pair was chosen over the alternatives for three reasons. The two
scenes are one day apart, so the diurnal comparison is not confounded
by a change in synoptic conditions. The daytime scene has 8.49\,\%
cloud cover and retains ample valid pixels after masking (reported
below). And the night-time scene's temperatures are physically
plausible for a Mumbai October night --- an earlier candidate pair in
November was rejected because its night-time values were anomalously
low, in a range inconsistent with the season.

\paragraph{Cloud masking.}

Both scenes were screened on \texttt{QA\_PIXEL}, masking bits 0--5:
fill, dilated cloud, cirrus, cloud, cloud shadow and snow. Bit~6
(clear) and bit~7 (water) were left unmasked --- water is handled
through WorldCover instead, so that the two parts treat it the same
way. Valid-pixel fractions after masking are given in
Table~\ref{tab:scenes} and discussed in Section~\ref{sec:landsat_results}.

\paragraph{From radiance to temperature.}

\paragraph{Daytime.} The Collection~2 Level-2 product supplies surface
temperature directly. \texttt{ST\_B10} was scaled by \num{0.00341802}
with an offset of \num{149.0} to give kelvin, then converted to
Celsius. The USGS single-channel retrieval behind this product applies
its own emissivity correction, derived from ASTER GED.

\paragraph{Night-time.} No Level-2 surface temperature is produced for
night acquisitions, so the Level-1 product was used. Band~10 digital
numbers were converted to spectral radiance using the scene's own
\texttt{RADIANCE\_MULT\_BAND\_10} and \texttt{RADIANCE\_ADD\_BAND\_10},
then inverted through the Planck function with
$K_1 = \num{774.8853}$ and $K_2 = \num{1321.0789}$:

\begin{equation}
  T_b = \frac{K_2}{\ln\!\left(\dfrac{K_1}{L_\lambda} + 1\right)}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The night-time figure is brightness temperature, not LST.}
No emissivity correction was applied to the night scene, so what
Part~A reports at night is $T_b$. Brightness temperature is lower than
land surface temperature wherever $\varepsilon < 1$, and the offset
differs between built-up ($\varepsilon \approx 0.97$) and vegetated
($\varepsilon \approx 0.98$--$0.99$) surfaces, so it does not cancel
in the urban-minus-rural subtraction. This matters when the night
figures are compared against MODIS in Section~\ref{sec:comparison},
where MODIS night LST \emph{is} emissivity-corrected.

Both brightness temperature and emissivity-corrected LST were
computed for the night scene, using a land-cover lookup for
emissivity. Table~\ref{tab:landsat_uhi} reports the night-time result
both ways: \SI{1.15}{\degreeCelsius} from brightness temperature and
\SI{2.13}{\degreeCelsius} from emissivity-corrected LST. The corrected
figure is treated as the night-time result throughout this report, and
the uncorrected one is retained for comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:landsatsens}.

A land-cover lookup was used rather than the NDVI threshold method
because NDVI requires the red and near-infrared bands, which carry no
signal at night. The values assigned follow standard tabulations:
\num{0.970} for built-up, \num{0.985} for vegetated surfaces,
\num{0.960} for bare ground and \num{0.990} for water. The daytime
scene needs no such treatment, since the Collection~2 Level-2 product
applies its own ASTER-GED-derived emissivity during retrieval --- so
day and night use different emissivity methods, which is a
consequence of what each observation makes available rather than a
choice between equals.

\paragraph{Statistics scale.}
\label{sec:landsat_scale}

The analysis extent --- the district plus the \SI{20}{\kilo\metre}
rural buffer --- covers about \SI{2800}{\kilo\metre\squared}, which at
the native \SI{30}{\metre} is roughly \num{3.1} billion pixels and
beyond what Earth Engine will reduce interactively. Zonal statistics
were computed at \SI{100}{\metre}, diagnostics at
\SIrange{200}{300}{\metre}, and raster exports left at
\SI{30}{\metre}. At \SI{100}{\metre} the Landsat statistics are still
ten times finer than the MODIS grid, so the resolution argument in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} is unaffected. The boundary was also
simplified to a \SI{100}{\metre} tolerance before the ring was built;
Part~B uses the unsimplified polygon, so the two rings differ by at
most \SI{100}{\metre} --- sub-pixel for both.

The day map shows the built-up core running along the peninsula and
into the eastern suburbs consistently warmer than the surrounding
green cover, matching the class ranking in
Table~\ref{tab:byclass}. The night map shows a similar but coarser
pattern with a visible striped gap in the north-west quadrant, most
likely the consequence of the unreliable night-time cloud mask
discussed above rather than a real feature of the surface. The
anomaly maps make the built-up footprint easier to trace than the raw
LST maps do, because they remove the broad north--south gradient
present in both scenes.

Day
and night UHI are close in magnitude on this pair,
\SI{2.39}{\degreeCelsius} and \SI{2.13}{\degreeCelsius}. That
similarity is specific to the date rather than general. October falls
in the post-monsoon season, and Part~B shows this is the one season in
which daytime and night-time UHI are both positive and of comparable
size (Terra: $+1.67$ and $+2.38$). A scene from pre-monsoon would have
given a very different daytime answer.

The two measurements also answer different questions. A single scene
captures one moment under one set of atmospheric conditions, including
whatever soil moisture and cloud history preceded it. A five-year
seasonal composite averages that away. Neither is more correct; the
Landsat figure is an instantaneous measurement at \SI{30}{\metre} and
the MODIS figure a climatological one at \SI{1}{\kilo\metre}.

Against the matched MODIS rows for post-monsoon 2015
(Table~\ref{tab:comparison}), the night-time agreement is close --- 
\SI{2.13}{\degreeCelsius} against Terra's \SI{2.09}{\degreeCelsius} --- 
while the daytime figures diverge, \SI{2.39}{\degreeCelsius} against
\SI{1.24}{\degreeCelsius}. The night agreement is the more surprising
of the two, since the two retrievals share almost nothing: different
sensors, different algorithms, different emissivity treatments,
different temporal sampling. Agreement to \SI{0.04}{\degreeCelsius}
under those conditions suggests the night-time signal is robust to
methodological choices in a way the daytime signal is not.

The distributions in Figure~\ref{fig:landsat_dist} show why. At night
the urban distribution is a narrow peak (sd \num{1.01}) sitting almost
entirely above the rural spread (sd \num{1.28}); nearly every urban
pixel is warmer than nearly every rural one. By day the two overlap
heavily despite a larger mean difference, so the daytime figure
depends much more on which pixels fall in each zone --- and therefore
on the zone definition, the pixel size, and the mixing that follows
from them.

Landsat UHI moves by at most \SI{0.18}{\degreeCelsius} across the four

Table~\ref{tab:ringsens}, which ranges up to
\SI{0.50}{\degreeCelsius}.

Landsat
is the less sensitive of the two despite the finer grid, which is at
first counter-intuitive. The explanation is temporal rather than
spatial. The MODIS sweep operates on a five-year seasonal mean in
which the land-cover gradient outside the city --- irrigated land
close in, drier land further out --- is consistently present, so
moving the ring systematically changes what the reference is made of.
The Landsat sweep operates on one October morning, when that gradient
was weaker: post-monsoon soil moisture was still widely distributed,
so land at \SI{2}{} and \SI{10}{\kilo\metre} was thermally more alike
than it would be in, say, April.

The result is a useful caution. A sensitivity test on a single scene
measures how much the answer depends on the zone definition
\emph{on that date}, not in general. Part~B's sweep, spanning five
years and four seasons, is the better guide to how much the choice
matters overall.

($+2.39 \rightarrow +5.64$) and nearly halves the night-time one
($+2.13 \rightarrow +1.20$). The effect is an order of magnitude
larger than the MODIS equivalent in Table~\ref{tab:watertest}, where
the largest shift was \SI{0.45}{\degreeCelsius}.

The
sign reversal matches the prediction and the MODIS result, but the
magnitude does not: \SI{3.25}{\degreeCelsius} by day here against a
MODIS maximum of \SI{0.45}{\degreeCelsius}.

Two things differ. First, averaging. The MODIS figure is a five-year
seasonal mean, and a water surface is almost the same temperature
every day of every year --- so in a multi-year composite the sea
contributes a stable, heavily-averaged value, while the land around it
varies and partially averages out. In a single scene the full
instantaneous land--sea contrast is present undiluted. On 8 October
the daytime mean of water was \SI{27.55}{\degreeCelsius} against
\SI{36.92}{\degreeCelsius} for built-up, a gap of nearly ten degrees
(Table~\ref{tab:byclass}).

Second, mixing. At \SI{1}{\kilo\metre} a coastal MODIS pixel is part
land and part sea, and its retrieved temperature is already a blend;
the land--sea boundary is smeared across the pixel grid before any
zonal statistic is taken. At \SI{100}{\metre} the boundary is sharp
and sea pixels enter the rural mean at their full, undiluted value. The
finer sensor is therefore \emph{more} exposed to the error, not less
--- which is worth stating plainly, because the intuition that higher
resolution is uniformly safer does not hold here.

\paragraph{Emissivity correction.}
Night-time UHI computed from brightness temperature is
\SI{1.15}{\degreeCelsius}; from emissivity-corrected LST it is
\SI{2.13}{\degreeCelsius}. Applying the correction nearly doubles the
result.

The correction does not cancel in the urban-minus-rural subtraction
because the two zones have different emissivities: built-up is
assigned \num{0.970} and vegetated surfaces \num{0.985}. A surface
with lower emissivity emits less at a given temperature, so correcting
for it raises the retrieved temperature more over the city than over
the countryside, widening the difference.

The
rule of thumb from the module material is roughly \SI{1}{\kelvin} of
LST error per \SI{1.5}{\percent} of emissivity error. The built and
vegetated values used here differ by \num{0.015}, that is
\SI{1.5}{\percent}, so a shift of order \SI{1}{\kelvin} between the
two zones is exactly what the rule predicts. The observed change in
UHI is \SI{0.98}{\degreeCelsius}, which is close enough to the
predicted magnitude to serve as a check that the correction has been
applied correctly rather than introducing an artefact.

The report treats the emissivity-corrected value,
\SI{2.13}{\degreeCelsius}, as its night-time result. Brightness
temperature is not a surface temperature: it is the temperature a
blackbody would need to be to emit the measured radiance, and real
surfaces emit less than a blackbody. Reporting $T_b$ as though it were
LST would understate the heat island by nearly a degree, and would
understate it unevenly, because the error scales with how far each
surface's emissivity sits below unity.

\subsection{Reconciling the two parts}
\label{sec:zonemismatch}

An earlier version of Part~A used non-built pixels \emph{inside} the
city boundary as its rural reference, and did not exclude water. That
is a different definition from Part~B's external ring, and it made the
two parts non-comparable. Part~A was therefore rerun with the external
\SIrange{5}{20}{\kilo\metre} ring, water and wetland excluded, so both
parts now use identical zones.

Moving
the reference outside the city changes both figures, but in opposite
directions: daytime UHI falls by \SI{1.43}{\degreeCelsius} while

produces both.

An internal reference is made of parks, maidans, mangrove fringe and
undeveloped plots embedded within the built fabric. By day these are
the coolest surfaces in the city --- vegetated, often irrigated,
shaded --- and are cooler than genuine countryside, which by October
is drying out. Using them as the reference therefore exaggerates the
daytime contrast. By night the same patches are warmed by the
surrounding built fabric and by the urban plume they sit inside, so
they are \emph{warmer} than true rural land, and using them suppresses
the night-time contrast. The internal reference is biased in whichever
direction makes the city look more like its surroundings at night and
less like them by day.

The corrected night-time figure, \SI{2.13}{\degreeCelsius}, agrees
with MODIS Terra night for post-monsoon 2015 at
\SI{2.09}{\degreeCelsius}. The two retrievals share almost no
methodology --- different sensor, resolution, retrieval algorithm and
emissivity treatment --- so agreement to
\SI{0.04}{\degreeCelsius} is meaningful corroboration rather than
coincidence. The original \SI{0.32}{\degreeCelsius} had no such
support, and its disagreement with MODIS was the first sign that the
reference definition was at fault.

\section{Part B --- Seasonal UHI from MODIS}
\label{sec:partB}
% =============================================================

\subsection{Method}

Seasonal LST comes from the MODIS eight-day composites,
\texttt{MODIS/061/MOD11A2} (Terra) \cite{mod11a2} and
\texttt{MODIS/061/MYD11A2} (Aqua) \cite{myd11a2}, both at
\SI{1}{\kilo\metre}. These are Collection~6.1 split-window retrievals
with land-cover-based emissivity.

The eight-day product was chosen over the daily one
(\texttt{MOD11A1}/\texttt{MYD11A1}) because the daily product leaves
every cloud gap to be handled by hand, and Mumbai has four months of
near-continuous monsoon cloud. The eight-day product applies a
consistent clear-sky selection instead. The cost is that it hides how
few days actually contributed, which is measured in
Section~\ref{sec:sampling}.

\paragraph{Terra and Aqua.}
Both platforms were used rather than one. Terra crosses at about
\texttt{10:30} and \texttt{22:30} local solar time; Aqua at about
\texttt{13:30} and \texttt{01:30}. Terra's morning pass is close to
Landsat~8's ($\approx$\texttt{10:30}), so the Terra daytime numbers
are the ones comparable with Part~A. Aqua sits nearer the daily
maximum and the pre-dawn minimum, so it better captures the true
extremes. The Terra--Aqua gap within a season isolates time of day
from season.

Observation times were read from the \texttt{Day\_view\_time} and
\texttt{Night\_view\_time} bands rather than assumed. Over the urban
zone they average \textbf{10:38} and \textbf{22:14} for Terra, and
\textbf{13:28} and \textbf{01:52} for Aqua. Terra's morning pass is
within about eight minutes of Landsat's.

\paragraph{Season definition.}

The brief asks for two or three seasons; the first three cover the
full range of surface moisture and insolation in Mumbai. Post-monsoon
was added because the Landsat pair falls in it, so the cross-sensor
comparison in Section~\ref{sec:comparison} has a matching season.

\paragraph{Why IMD and not DJF/MAM/JJA.}
December--January--February for winter is a mid-latitude convention
and does not describe a tropical monsoon coast. Mumbai's year is set
by the onset and withdrawal of the southwest monsoon, not the
solstices. December still carries post-monsoon soil moisture and is
not thermally like January, which is why IMD places it in
post-monsoon. And no IMD season crosses a calendar year, so each year
gives exactly one clean instance of each season --- which is what
makes the year-by-year analysis in Section~\ref{sec:interannual}
possible. A DJF winter would straddle two years and force an arbitrary
choice about each December.

\paragraph{Climatological basis.}
IMD's revised normal dates for Mumbai place monsoon onset at
11~June and withdrawal at 8~October \cite{imd_onset_withdrawal},
which is what fixes the June--September window used for the monsoon
season here: it is bounded by the city's own onset and withdrawal
dates rather than a generic national figure. The pre-monsoon window is
the driest and hottest part of the year at this station --- the
1991--2020 normal gives a May mean daily maximum of
\SI{34.3}{\degreeCelsius} against \SI{31.2}{\degreeCelsius} in
January, with December--February rainfall below
\SI{2}{\milli\metre} a month against \SI{384}{} to
\SI{920}{\milli\metre} a month from June to September
\cite{imd_climate_normals}. Pre-monsoon (March--May) and winter
(January--February) are therefore climatologically distinct: the
former is hot and dry, building toward the monsoon onset, and the
latter is markedly cooler and equally dry.

\paragraph{Quality screening.}
\label{sec:qc}

Pixels were screened on \texttt{QC\_Day} and \texttt{QC\_Night}, keeping
mandatory QA (bits 0--1) of 0 or 1 --- not cloud-flagged, not otherwise
unproduced --- and LST error (bits 6--7) of 2 or better, meaning an
average retrieval error at or below \SI{3}{\kelvin}.

The eight-day product is already cloud-screened, so this is not cloud
masking. It removes retrievals produced under marginal conditions with
large stated errors, which in a monsoon climate cluster in the season
of most interest.

A stricter setting (mandatory QA $=0$, LST error
$\leq\,$\SI{2}{\kelvin}) was tried first and emptied some night-time
zones entirely, producing null zonal means. The relaxed setting was
used throughout. Pixel counts never fall below 239 urban and 917
rural.

\paragraph{Compositing.}
LST bands were scaled by \num{0.02} to kelvin, then
\SI{273.15}{\kelvin} subtracted. View-time bands were scaled by
\num{0.1} to local solar hours. For each platform, mode and season,
all qualifying composites from 2019--2023 were averaged per pixel.
Zonal means were taken at \SI{1000}{\metre}, the native MODIS grid
spacing, so no resampling precedes the statistics.

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{UHI} = \overline{T_{\mathrm{urban}}} - \overline{T_{\mathrm{rural}}}
\end{equation}

The urban zone holds 241 valid pixels at \SI{1}{\kilo\metre} and the
rural zone 917--1055. The urban count is the binding constraint on
precision.

Night-time UHI is positive in every season on both platforms, from

positive only in post-monsoon ($+1.67$ on both). A negative value
means the rural surface is hotter than the urban one --- a surface
urban \emph{cool} island.

\paragraph{Why daytime UHI is negative in winter and pre-monsoon.}
The urban means barely move between winter and pre-monsoon
(\SI{32.10}{} to \SI{36.38}{\degreeCelsius}, a rise of
\SI{4.28}{\degreeCelsius}) while the rural means rise much further
(\SI{32.50}{} to \SI{39.10}{\degreeCelsius}, a rise of
\SI{6.60}{\degreeCelsius}). The city has not cooled; the countryside
has heated past it. By the height of the pre-monsoon the rural surface
is dry bare soil and senescent vegetation with low thermal inertia and
almost no soil moisture left to evaporate, so incoming radiation goes
almost entirely into raising the surface temperature. The built
surface has higher thermal inertia and conducts heat into the mass of
the material below, so for the same radiation load its surface warms
less. The result is a surface urban \emph{cool} island of
$-2.72$\,\si{\degreeCelsius}.

\paragraph{Why night-time UHI is positive in every season.}
The same thermal inertia that limits daytime urban warming sustains
the night-time heat island. Heat conducted into building fabric and
pavement during the day is released slowly after sunset, while the
rural surface, having stored little, cools rapidly by radiation. The
diurnal ranges make this explicit: the urban zone falls
\SI{9.87}{\degreeCelsius} between the Terra day and night overpasses
in winter against \SI{13.07}{\degreeCelsius} for rural, and
\SI{10.18}{} against \SI{14.81}{\degreeCelsius} in pre-monsoon. The
city is thermally more stable in both directions --- slower to heat
and slower to cool --- which is the definition of higher apparent
thermal inertia. This is the nocturnal dominance Oke describes
\cite{oke}, and it holds regardless of what the daytime signal is
doing.

\paragraph{Monsoon.}
Daytime UHI is near zero in monsoon ($+0.07$ Terra, $+0.92$ Aqua).
When both surfaces are wet, evaporation dominates the energy balance
on both sides of the boundary and the thermal contrast between them
largely disappears --- the rural surface can no longer run away from
the city because its water is not exhausted. This reading should be
treated with caution: as Section~\ref{sec:sampling} shows, only
13.3\,\% of monsoon retrievals survive screening, so the composite
describes the clearest days of the monsoon rather than the monsoon
itself.

\paragraph{Terra against Aqua.}
The platforms agree closely in some season/mode combinations and
diverge in others. The largest daytime gap is monsoon ($+0.07$ against
$+0.92$, \SI{0.85}{\degreeCelsius} apart) and the largest night-time
gap is pre-monsoon ($+1.90$ against $+2.53$,
\SI{0.63}{\degreeCelsius}). Since the only difference between the two
is roughly three hours of clock time, these gaps measure how fast the
urban--rural contrast is changing at those hours. Aqua's larger
daytime monsoon figure is consistent with its later overpass catching
the surface nearer its daily maximum, when the drying of exposed
surfaces has had longer to act. The implication for reporting is that
a single-platform seasonal UHI is a statement about a particular time
of day, not about the season as a whole.

\paragraph{The day--night reversal.}
Daytime UHI is negative in two of four seasons while night-time UHI is
positive in all four. Daytime UHI is
negative in two of four seasons while night-time UHI is positive in
all four. The heat island is therefore not a fixed property of the
city: it reverses sign by day depending on how dry the surrounding
countryside is, and persists only at night. The two behaviours have
the same physical cause --- the higher thermal inertia of built
surfaces --- expressed in opposite directions at opposite ends of the
diurnal cycle.

The diurnal ranges confirm this directly. Between the Terra day and
night overpasses the urban zone changes by \SI{9.87}{},
\SI{10.18}{}, \SI{8.38}{} and \SI{8.11}{\degreeCelsius} across the
four seasons, against \SI{13.07}{}, \SI{14.81}{}, \SI{10.03}{} and
\SI{8.82}{\degreeCelsius} for the rural zone. The urban range is
narrower in every season, by as much as
\SI{4.62}{\degreeCelsius} in pre-monsoon. A surface that neither heats
nor cools as far as its surroundings will appear cooler than them by
day and warmer by night, which is what the seasonal table shows.

The

enough for a retrieval to be produced and pass screening. Those are by
construction the least monsoon-like days of the season: the ones with
broken cloud, more incoming radiation and drier surfaces than the
seasonal norm.

The bias therefore runs toward the pre-monsoon condition. On a genuine
monsoon day both surfaces would be wet and the contrast between them
smaller still, so the true monsoon UHI is likely nearer zero, or more
negative, than the $+0.07$\,\si{\degreeCelsius} retrieved. The
retrieved value is an upper bound on the daytime monsoon heat island
rather than an estimate of it.

The monsoon row in Table~\ref{tab:headline} should accordingly carry
the least weight of the four. This is not a defect of the product but
a limit of thermal infrared remote sensing: the instrument cannot see
the surface through cloud, so no amount of compositing recovers the
cloudy days. The interannual spread in
Section~\ref{sec:interannual} shows the same instability from a
different angle.

\SI{10}{\kilo\metre} shifts UHI by up to
\SI{0.50}{\degreeCelsius}. Pushing the outer edge from
\SI{20}{\kilo\metre} to \SI{30}{\kilo\metre} changes almost nothing
(\num{0.01} to \SI{0.03}{\degreeCelsius}).

Moving the inner
edge outward makes UHI more negative in every season: winter goes from
$-0.29$ to $-0.58$, pre-monsoon from $-2.55$ to
$-3.05$\,\si{\degreeCelsius}. The rural mean rises as the ring moves
away from the city, which means land nearer the boundary is cooler
than land further out. Read one way this is the city's thermal
influence reaching past its edge and warming the near-ring; read
another, it reflects a land-cover gradient, with more irrigated and
vegetated land immediately outside the built-up fringe. Both would
produce the same signal, and this data cannot separate them.

Either reading supports keeping a gap between city and reference. The
\SI{5}{\kilo\metre} inner edge sits at the point where the trend is
already flattening --- moving from \SI{2}{} to \SI{5}{\kilo\metre}
changes UHI by \SI{0.17}{\degreeCelsius} in pre-monsoon, while
\SI{5}{} to \SI{10}{\kilo\metre} changes it by
\SI{0.33}{\degreeCelsius} --- so it excludes the most contaminated
land without pushing the reference into a different landscape.

The outer edge barely matters: extending from \SI{20}{} to
\SI{30}{\kilo\metre} moves UHI by at most
\SI{0.03}{\degreeCelsius}. The asymmetry says the useful rural land is
concentrated in the inner part of the ring. Land added beyond
\SI{20}{\kilo\metre} is either sea, or so similar to what is already
included that it does not shift the mean.

Compared against the Landsat sweep in
Table~\ref{tab:landsat_ring}, which moves by at most
\SI{0.18}{\degreeCelsius}, MODIS is the more sensitive of the two
despite its coarser grid. The likely reason is temporal rather than
spatial: the MODIS figure is a five-year seasonal mean in which the
land-cover gradient outside the city is consistently present, while
the single Landsat scene carries one day's conditions in which that
gradient may be weaker.

Every daytime difference is positive (\num{+0.10} to \num{+0.45}).

The signs behave as
predicted in Section~\ref{sec:waterexcl}. Every daytime difference is
positive, meaning that including water inflates the apparent heat
island, and three of four night-time differences are negative, meaning
it suppresses it. The reversal confirms the mechanism: the sea is
cooler than land by day and warmer by night, so it pulls the rural
mean in opposite directions at the two overpasses.

The daytime effect is about five times the night-time one --- up to
\SI{0.45}{} against \SI{0.09}{\degreeCelsius}. This follows from the
diurnal ranges. Water's very high heat capacity means its surface
temperature moves only slightly between day and night, while land
swings by \SIrange{9}{15}{\degreeCelsius}. The land--water contrast is
therefore large at the daytime overpass and small at the night-time
one, so mixing water into the rural zone displaces the daytime mean
much further.

Whether this changes any conclusion depends on the season. The
seasonal ordering in Table~\ref{tab:headline} survives: pre-monsoon
remains strongly negative at $-2.28$ and post-monsoon strongly
positive at $+1.77$. But winter shifts from $-0.41$ to $-0.12$, close
enough to zero that the sign becomes a matter of measurement error
rather than physics. Excluding water is therefore necessary rather
than merely tidy, and the necessity is sharpest exactly where the
signal is weakest.

\subsection{Accounting for the disagreement}

The night-time
figures agree closely: Landsat \SI{2.13}{} against Terra
\SI{2.09}{\degreeCelsius}. The daytime figures do not: Landsat
\SI{2.39}{} against Terra \SI{1.24}{\degreeCelsius}, a gap of
\SI{1.15}{\degreeCelsius}. The question is what explains the daytime
gap while leaving the night-time agreement intact.

\paragraph{Resolution.} At \SI{1}{\kilo\metre} a MODIS pixel over
Mumbai mixes built-up with vegetation, water and open ground, and the
retrieved temperature is a blend. Mixing compresses the urban--rural
contrast, so MODIS should read lower than Landsat --- and it does. The
class means in Table~\ref{tab:byclass} give a sense of the scale:
built-up at \SI{36.92}{} against tree cover at
\SI{32.04}{\degreeCelsius}, so a pixel that is half each would read
roughly \SI{2.4}{\degreeCelsius} below pure built-up. Mixing is
therefore capable of explaining a gap of this size on its own.

\paragraph{Overpass time.} Terra's observed daytime overpass is
\texttt{10:41} and Landsat's is near \texttt{10:30}, about eleven
minutes apart. The Terra--Aqua difference for the same season is
\SI{0.44}{\degreeCelsius} across three hours, so eleven minutes
accounts for a few hundredths of a degree at most. Time of day is not
the explanation.

\paragraph{Temporal sampling.} Landsat is one scene on 8 October;
the matched MODIS row is a post-monsoon composite for the whole of
2015, October to December. Even the matched row therefore averages two
months the Landsat scene never saw, including December, when the
land--city contrast differs from October's. This is a real
contribution and is impossible to remove without a daily product.

\paragraph{Retrieval and emissivity.} MOD11A2 uses a generalised
split-window with land-cover-class emissivity; Landsat Collection~2
uses a single-channel retrieval with ASTER-GED emissivity. At roughly
\SI{1}{\kelvin} per \SI{1.5}{\percent} of emissivity error, systematic
differences between the two emissivity sources could contribute a few
tenths of a degree.

\paragraph{Which dominates.} Resolution is the largest single
contributor, with temporal sampling second. The night-time agreement
supports this reading: at night the urban distribution is narrow and
uniformly elevated (Figure~\ref{fig:landsat_dist}), so a mixed pixel
averages values that are all similar and mixing costs little. By day
the urban distribution is broad and overlaps the rural one, so mixing
pulls the urban mean down substantially. The same resolution
difference costs little at night and a great deal by day --- which is
exactly the pattern observed.

\section{Limitations}
% =============================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item WorldCover 2021 is used to classify 2015 Landsat observations,
        so the urban mask is an approximation of the 2015 extent.
        Mumbai's built-up
extent grew between 2015 and 2021, so some pixels classed as built-up
in the 2021 product were not built in 2015. Those pixels would have
been cooler by day than genuine 2015 built-up, so the urban mean is
biased slightly low and the Part~A daytime UHI is, if anything, a
slight underestimate.
  \item The Landsat night-time variable is brightness temperature, not
        a fully retrieved LST.
  \item Part~A rests on one daytime and one night-time scene, so the
        values carry that day's weather rather than a climatology.
  \item Both parts measure surface temperature, not near-surface air
        temperature. The two differ substantially over built surfaces
        by day.
  \item The monsoon
composite retains only 13.3\,\% of available retrievals, so it
describes the clearest days of the monsoon rather than the season as
a whole (Section~\ref{sec:sampling}). The monsoon figures should be
read as an upper bound on the daytime heat island, not an estimate of
it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================

Mumbai's surface urban heat island
is not one number. By day it is strongly seasonal and changes sign:
Terra MODIS gives $-2.72$\,\si{\degreeCelsius} in pre-monsoon, when
the dry countryside outheats the city, and $+1.67$\,\si{\degreeCelsius}
in post-monsoon. By night it is positive in every season, between
$+1.38$ and $+2.80$\,\si{\degreeCelsius}. Both behaviours follow from
the same property --- built surfaces have higher thermal inertia than
their surroundings --- expressed at opposite ends of the diurnal
cycle. The urban diurnal range is narrower than the rural one in every
season, by up to \SI{4.62}{\degreeCelsius}.

The most robust result is the night-time heat island. It is positive
in all four seasons and on both platforms, it survives every
sensitivity test applied, and the Landsat and MODIS estimates for the
matched season and year agree to \SI{0.04}{\degreeCelsius} despite
sharing no methodology. The least robust is the monsoon daytime
figure, where only 13.3\,\% of retrievals survive screening and the
sign changes three times across five years.

The sensitivity tests carry a lesson beyond their individual numbers.
Moving the rural reference from inside the city to an external ring
changed the night-time result by \SI{0.80}{\degreeCelsius} and the
daytime result by \SI{1.43}{\degreeCelsius}. Admitting water to the
rural zone changed the Landsat daytime figure by
\SI{3.25}{\degreeCelsius}. Both are larger than the differences
between the sensors this report set out to compare. A UHI value quoted
without its zone definition is not interpretable.

A follow-up would do three things differently. It would use a daily
MODIS product rather than the eight-day composite, so the monsoon
sampling bias could be quantified per day rather than inferred from
retention rates. It would use a land-cover product contemporaneous
with each thermal observation, rather than applying a 2021
classification to 2015 imagery. And it would seek additional
night-time Landsat acquisitions, if any exist for Mumbai, since the
entire Part~A night-time result rests on a single scene whose cloud
screening cannot be verified.
